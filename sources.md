# Sources

The frozen bibliography. Each entry as it appears in the paper's `## References`, with a one-line provenance note. Every in-text `et al.` maps to a co-authored entry below.

## Bibliography

Acemoglu, D., Aghion, P., Bursztyn, L., and Hemous, D. (2012). The environment and directed technical change. *American Economic Review*, 102(1), 131–166. — directed technical change and clean-tech cost feedback; grounds the learning term and threshold stickiness.

Arthur, W. B. (1989). Competing technologies, increasing returns, and lock-in by historical events. *Economic Journal*, 99(394), 116–131. — increasing returns and technological lock-in; the bistability mechanism.

David, P. A. (1985). Clio and the economics of QWERTY. *American Economic Review*, 75(2), 332–337. — path dependence; lock-in as a historical basin.

Goldthau, A., and Sitter, N. (2015). *A Liberal Actor in a Realist World: The European Union Regulatory State and the Global Political Economy of Energy*. Oxford University Press. — energy as political economy; background for the geopolitics of taps.

Hirschman, A. O. (1945). *National Power and the Structure of Foreign Trade*. University of California Press. — dependence as leverage; molecule power as chokepoint power.

Hofbauer, J., and Sigmund, K. (1998). *Evolutionary Games and Population Dynamics*. Cambridge University Press. — replicator dynamics; the dynamical form of system substitution.

International Energy Agency. (2025). *Electricity 2025*. IEA. — electrification shares (China ~0.28, US ~0.22, EU ~0.21); sets x0 and the early-transition regime.

Nagy, B., Farmer, J. D., Bui, Q. M., and Trancik, J. E. (2013). Statistical basis for predicting technological progress. *PLoS ONE*, 8(2), e52669. — experience curves; the learning regularity behind the cost decline.

Overland, I., Bazilian, M., Ilimbek Uulu, T., Vakulchuk, R., and Westphal, K. (2019). The GeGaLo index: Geopolitical gains and losses after energy transition. *Energy Strategy Reviews*, 26, 100406. — quantifying post-transition geopolitical redistribution; the electron-power literature.

Scholten, D. (Ed.). (2018). *The Geopolitics of Renewables*. Springer. — the foundational collection on renewable-energy geopolitics.

Scholten, D., and Bosman, R. (2016). The geopolitics of renewables: Exploring the political implications of renewable energy systems. *Technological Forecasting and Social Change*, 103, 273–283. — why electron power differs structurally from molecule power.

Tooze, A. (2025). Chartbook 439: Electrostates versus petrostates. *Chartbook*. — the petrostate/electrostate ideal-type contrast the paper sharpens; the molecule/electron vocabulary.

Unruh, G. C. (2000). Understanding carbon lock-in. *Energy Policy*, 28(12), 817–830. — carbon lock-in as co-evolution of technology, institutions, infrastructure; the bistability in words.

Way, R., Ives, M. C., Mealy, P., and Farmer, J. D. (2022). Empirically grounded technology forecasts and the energy transition. *Joule*, 6(9), 2057–2082. — learning curves imply a fast, cheap transition; the global cost decline that bounds the delay.

Yergin, D. (2011). *The Quest: Energy, Security, and the Remaking of the Modern World*. Penguin Press. — energy security and the politics of supply; background for molecule power.
